Significant efforts are currently being directed toward the implementation of large-scale quantum
information processors, but realizing useful quantum advantage also requires the development of
efficient quantum algorithms. While for many practical problems the dominant resource costs
are associated with a small set of computational primitives, optimizing them in the quantum
setting presents unique challenges, including areversibilityconstraint absent from ordinary classical
computation. Here we introduce an evolutionary framework in which large language models (LLMs)
propose circuit designs and an independent evaluator verifies and scores them. We apply this
framework to tasks spanning numerical computation, chemistry, optimization, quantum simulation,
and number theory. In seven of the eight tasks, the discovered circuits significantly reducebothToffoli
and qubit counts relative to the state of the art. In the eighth, the search discovers a new reversible
architecture for prime-field modular inversion. By handling reversibility differently, the architecture
makes divsteps—a classical algorithm previously impractical for reversible integer arithmetic—viable
and substantially improves the Toffoli–qubit trade-off for modular inversion. These results open the
door for evaluator-guided LLM co-design of new algorithmic structures for quantum computation.
Computational primitives based on classical function
evaluation play a central role in a wide range of quantum
algorithms. Such primitives include decoding routines for
optimization [1], numerical functions for quantum simula-
tion [2], arithmetic routines for number theory [3], and
quantum read-only memory (QROM), which coherently
loads classically specified data into quantum registers [4].
To operate on quantum data, these functions must be
compiled into circuits that can be executed coherently on
quantum hardware. This imposes two constraints absent
from ordinary classical programs. First, because the in-
put may be in superposition, the circuit cannot inspect
an intermediate value and select an ordinary classical
control-flow path. Data-dependent behavior is instead
expressed through controlled operations within a fixed
gate sequence, so every branch contributes to the circuit’s
cost even if it applies only to some inputs. Second, the
computation must be reversible: intermediate information
cannot simply be overwritten or discarded. Temporary
values must eventually be uncomputed, returning their
qubits to their initial state and preventing residual infor-
mation from remaining entangled with the output. Until
they are uncomputed, these values occupy additional
qubits. At the same time, when bounded error is permit-
ted, machinery needed only for rare or atypical inputs can
sometimes be omitted, reducing resource requirements
while keeping the failure probability controlled. Efficient
coherent compilation is of major practical importance,
and recent reductions in resource requirements have come
from coordinated changes to arithmetic algorithms, data
representations, and their reversible implementation [5, 6].
Such redesigns are difficult for general quantum circuits

Here we introduce a framework that pairs large lan-
guage model (LLM) proposals with an independent eval-
uator that verifies and scores each candidate. We focus
on a class of quantum circuits that compute a classical
function f: for an arbitrary input|ψ⟩ = Px,y αx,y |x⟩ |y⟩,
the circuit must act as|ψ⟩ 7− →Px,y αx,y |x⟩ |y⊕f(x)⟩,
where |x⟩ and |y⟩ are computational-basis states of the
input and output registers, respectively. By linear-
ity, this coherent action is correct if the circuit per-
forms the required map on every computational-basis
input: |x⟩ |y⟩ 7− → |x⟩ |y⊕f(x)⟩. This basis-state re-
duction makes candidate circuits amenable to program-
matic evaluation, since every allowed operation maps a
computational-basis state to another computational-basis
state, possibly changing its phase, but never creating a
superposition [8]. The action of a candidate on any chosen
input can therefore be simulated by tracking a bit string
and its phase, rather than2n amplitudes. Resource ac-
counting is also simple: we use the Toffoli countT and the
peak qubit countQ, and rank circuits by their product,
the T Q-cost. Together, these properties let us build an
efficient, programmaticevaluator that checks the correct-
ness and resource cost of any candidate circuit design.

Across eight diverse compilation tasks, the discovered
circuits repeatedly exhibit three common principles of
algorithm–circuit co-design. These principles address the
features of coherent compilation described above: express-
ing data-dependent control flow, making intermediate
computations reversible without retaining their entire his-
tory, and using a failure budget to remove circuit resources
devoted to atypical cases (see Figure 1).

DESIGN PRINCIPLES
One recurring principle in the circuits discovered by
the search is to replace conditional arithmetic withcondi-
tional routing. A naïve coherent translation of a casewise
classical algorithm includes a separately controlled arith-
metic block for every branch. The discovered circuits
instead use a representation in which the branches differ
primarily in which values occupy particular computational
roles. Relatively cheap controlled swaps then route the
appropriate values into those roles, one unconditional
arithmetic path performs the expensive work, and further
routing places the results in their required registers. In
this sense, where a classical program routes execution
to the selected operation, the coherent circuit routes the
selected data through a shared operation. The idea re-
sembles bucket-brigade quantum random-access memory
(QRAM) [11].
For example, consider fourn-qubit fixed-point registers
c, x, y, and z, together with a control qubitb. Suppose
that we wish to perform

routing instead swaps y and z according to b, applies
one uncontrolled multiply-add, and undoes the swap.

More generally, conditional routing requirescanonical-
izingbranch-dependent states into a representation on
which the same expensive computation applies. Schemati-
cally, when the branch can be expressed asUb = R−1b KR b,
the circuit need only control the inexpensive routing trans-
formation Rb; the expensive kernelK appears once and
need not be controlled. Finding such a decomposition
typically takes more than recognizing identical arithmetic
in two branches: it exploits an equivalence in the required
output so that apparently different updates become in-
stances of the same computation. The difficulty lies less
in the routing circuit than in determining which branch-
dependent differences matter and which can be absorbed
into a shared representation.
Reversibility creates a second challenge: reversing a
conditional update requires knowing which branch was
taken during the forward computation. A naïve imple-
mentation records each decision in a history stack whose
size grows with the number of updates. But the branch
decisions need not always be stored explicitly: different
branches may leave distinguishable patterns in registers
that remain available during uncomputation. As illus-
trated in Figure 1(c), the circuit can recover the branch
bit from such a pattern, use it to select the appropriate
inverse update, and then clear the recovered bit, so the
register contents serve as an inexpensive record of the
branch decisions. For example, suppose we have a register
g that begins atg0 = 0. In round j, conditioned on some
branch bitb j, we add2j, so that
gj+1 =g j +b j2j.(2)
For arbitrary values ofgj, there is no way to tell from
gj+1 which branch was taken. Globally, however, there
is an important invariant:gj < 2j, and therefore bitj of
gj+1 must equal bj. During uncomputation, the circuit
can simply read this bit to recoverbj. The computation’s
evolving state thus supplies the branch decisions in reverse
order without a separate history stack. In general, finding
such a decoder requires the forward algorithm, its data
representation, and its uncomputation procedure to be
designed together. Viewed locally, an update may be
ambiguous, since either branch can appear to admit a
valid predecessor. The ambiguity can disappear once the
circuit is restricted to states reachable from the prescribed
initialization, where a relation across the registers can
distinguish histories that no single register can.
The third principle exploits the small but finite fail-
ure probability permitted by most quantum algorithms.
Turning this failure budget into resource savings, how-
ever, is not automatic. One must first identify the rare
inputs that determine worst-case resource requirements.
Unlike a classical program, a quantum circuit must con-
tain the gates and ancillas required by every supported
path. A rare input can therefore determine the circuit’s
Toffoli count or peak qubit count. Yet identifying such
inputs is not enough: the circuitry supporting them may
not be separable from the computation on typical inputs.
The co-design task is to reorganize the computation so
that the additional resources required by rare inputs are
localized—for example, to extra iterations, stack entries,
or arithmetic lanes. Those components can then be omit-
ted while confining failure to the low-probability inputs
that require them (Figure 1(d)). In what follows we
show how these design principles result in significantly
improved algorithmic circuits across a wide variety of
quantum tasks.
REVERSIBLE SYNDROME DECODING FOR DQI
Decoded quantum interferometry (DQI) is a quantum
algorithm that turns certain optimization problems into
decoding problems for classical error-correcting codes,
using quantum interference to favor better solutions. A
central application is optimal polynomial intersection
(OPI), a form of polynomial fitting over finite fields for
which DQI offers a superpolynomial speedup over known
classical algorithms. DQI achieves this by using a Reed–
Solomon decoder as a subroutine within the quantum
computation [1]. The decoder identifies the positions and
values of errors from a collection of algebraic checks called
syndromes. DQI requires this decoding to run coherently
onasuperpositionofinputs, makingthereversibledecoder
a dominant resource cost. Khattaret al.[13] compiled

reversible Reed–Solomon decoders based on the extended
Euclidean algorithm.
We study their primary Reed–Solomon instance, in

pattern containing at most 35 errors. Our search frame-

REVERSIBLE MODULAR INVERSION
Modular inversion is the task of findingx such that
wx≡ 1 ( modp ), given a nonzero inputw and a primep.
It is a central arithmetic subroutine in a range of quantum
algorithms [3, 14]. A standard way to compute the inverse
is through the extended Euclidean algorithm. Euclidean
algorithms repeatedly simplify a pair of integers while
preserving their greatest common divisor. By tracking
howthisdivisorisexpressedintermsoftheoriginalinputs,
they also recover coefficientsα, βsatisfying αp + βw = 1,
soβis the inverse ofwmodulop.
Divsteps is a variant of the Euclidean algorithm that
replaces division with conditional additions, subtractions,
and halving [15]. Starting fromf = p and g = w, the goal
is to reduce the pair tof = ±1and g = 0while preserving
their greatest common divisor. The basic strategy is to
hold the odd integerf fixed while reducingg: if g is even,
it can be halved directly; ifg is odd, adding or subtracting
f first makes it even. These operations preserve the great-
est common divisor becausef is odd. Holding f fixed
indefinitely, however, would leave only one integer being
reduced, so the algorithm must periodically swap their
roles. A small signed counter z schedules these swaps
without ever comparingf and g: it roughly tracks how
many more bitsf has than g, so a swap occurs onceg is

Further savings are possible when the application per-
mits a small failure probability. We use this failure budget
to exclude rare, unusually demanding inputs through two
resource limits: a fixed history-stack size and a fixed win-
dow of supported stopping times. The history-stack limit
builds on the decoding trade-off. Withjstore = 5, only
12.4%of ambiguous decisions are written to the stack
(Figure 3(a)). On average, the required stack size is 21
history qubits, but it can reach 263 in the worst case. We
instead reserve 60 and allow the circuit to fail on rare
inputs for which the stack overflows. Such overflows occur
with probability2 .4 × 10−6 per inversion (Figure 3(b)),
saving 203 history qubits relative to the worst case. The
stopping-time limit addresses a separate cost. A fixed
quantum circuit must schedule enough steps for every
input it supports. Although 590 divsteps suffice even in
the worst case, stopping times are concentrated in a much
narrower range (Figure 3(b)). The discovered circuit
therefore schedules 533 steps and tests for termination
only from step 499 onward; inputs that terminate outside
this window are allowed to fail.
Branch decoding and bounded resource allocation thus
make divsteps practical for reversible modular inversion.
A complete benchmark circuit built on this inversion
(SI II) uses 4.0 million Toffolis and 935 qubits.

METHODS
A classical function cannot generally be applied directly within a quantum algorithm, because quantum evolution
must be reversible. Instead, a functionf is embedded in a reversible map|x⟩ |y⟩ 7− → |x⟩ |y⊕f(x)⟩. A natural model for
these subroutines is thekickmixcircuit model of Babbushet al. [8], which augments reversible classical computation
with measurement-based uncomputation (MBU) and diagonal sign gates. Despite these additional operations,kickmix
circuits remain efficiently classically simulable. This allows candidate circuits to be tested for their action on coherent
quantum superpositions, providing the reliable correctness signal needed for evolutionary search.
Circuits that implement the same specification can have very different time and space requirements. We use Toffoli
count and peak qubit count as proxies for these costs. Toffoli gates require expensive non-Clifford resources, so their
count tracks a large part of the logical work and often the runtime [24, 25]. The peak qubit count measures the data
and ancillas that must coexist during execution and therefore approximates the required logical processor footprint [26].
The two resources also typically trade against each other: additional ancillas can avoid recomputation, while recycling
ancillas can reduce space at the cost of additional gates. We multiply these quantities to obtain theT Q-cost, which
provides a single objective for comparing such designs.
Together, the correctness tests andT Q-cost define a constrained optimization problem over circuit-generating
programs. Motivated by recent advances in coding and reasoning agents and LLM-guided algorithmic discovery, we
use an LLM to propose coordinated, task-specific redesigns within an evolutionary search [20, 27, 28].
Circuit model and validity
A compilation task specifies a reversible action on computational-basis states: a permutationπ and, when needed, a
Boolean phaseg. A circuit may useaancilla qubits, but must return them to zero:
V|x⟩ |0a⟩= (−1) g(x) |π(x)⟩ |0a⟩.(10)
Every ancilla must return to|0⟩ because quantum operations must be unitary, and thus reversible. For a numerical
approximation, the required output relation may instead specify an error bound; interface preservation, phase
correctness, and ancilla uncomputation remain exact. We separately allow some circuits to fail on a bounded fraction
of inputs drawn from a stated distribution; this fraction is thefailure budget. Following Babbushet al.[8], thekickmix
model permits three classes of operation:
1. classical reversible gates:X, CNOT, Toffoli, and swap;
2. diagonal sign gates:Z, CZ, CCZ, and global sign flips; and
3. destructiveX-basis measurements.

string, while diagonal gates update only its sign, so the state remains of the form(−1)ϕ |x⟩. A classical simulator
therefore need only track the current bitsx and the signϕ, rather than a full state vector. The operations apply phase
kickback whilemixing basis states, givingkickmixits name.

Validation and statistical guarantees
The circuits are ultimately applied to coherent superpositions, whereas the evaluator follows one computational-basis
trajectory at a time. Each test must therefore capture enough information to determine the trajectory’s coherent
action. For a basis inputx and measurement recordm, the evaluator checks that the output matches the target,
required inputs are unchanged, every ancilla has returned to|0⟩, and the phase agrees with the target exactly. These
checks compare the complete final state rather than only its classical output. An ancilla that retains input-dependent
information can become entangled with the logical registers, while an incorrect sign can alter subsequent interference
without changing any output bit. Resetting or tracing out a dirty ancilla is therefore not a substitute for uncomputing
it.
If every pair(x, m)passes these tests, the measurement record factors from the logical output, and linearity extends
agreement on every trajectory to any coherent input. For large input domains, however, the evaluator samples these
trajectories rather than checking them all.

Cost model and scoring
We define theT Q-cost of a circuit by
T Q=Toffoli count×peak qubit count,(22)
whereTcounts Toffoli and CCZ gates equally andQis the largest number of logical qubits allocated at once.
We weight space and time equally in this cost function for simplicity. In practice, their relative importance depends
on the hardware and application. When qubits are scarce, for example, a cost of the form(qubit count)κ ×Toffoli count
with κ >1may be more appropriate; resource estimates for factoring have usedκ between 1 and 3 [6, 30]. Either T or
Q can also be replaced with other measures of time or space. In particular, when there is limited magic-state-factory
throughput (as is typically the case), runtime is bottlenecked by the Toffoli count, motivating its widespread use as a
time proxy [24, 25]. However, with enough factories to supply magic states in parallel, Toffoli depth rather than Toffoli
count limits runtime. Similarly, for architectures with cheap cold storage for inactive qubits [31], Q can be replaced by
active volume [32], which counts only the qubits participating in logical operations.
The search is not tied to the specific form of theT Q-cost: it can run with any computable objective. A later case study
illustrates the available trade-offs: the modular-inversion search (SI II) produces a family of circuits along a Toffoli–qubit
frontier (SI II.1), and an objective that weights qubit count more heavily selects a lower-qubit member of that family.
Evolutionary search
We encode each candidate as Python code that constructs a circuit. Functions, loops, and named registers preserve
the organization of the computation, allowing the LLM to reason about arithmetic subroutines and their composition
without reconstructing that structure from a flattened gate sequence. For example, changing a shared arithmetic
routine changes every place where it is used, while reorganizing calls to that routine can change when intermediate
values are computed, retained, and uncomputed. The representation thus makes coordinated changes expressible as
revisions to the underlying algorithm.
We search over these circuit-generating programs with OpenEvolve [33], using Claude Mythos 5 [28] to propose
architectures and program rewrites. Each of the eight searches uses a fixed, hand-written task specification, and all
share the same generic circuit-building interface. During ideation, the LLM receives only the specification, circuit
model, and correctness criteria, and proposes several distinct circuit architectures. The LLM has no access to the
evaluator beyond the feedback returned after each submission. Starting from a blank program, it chooses the algorithm,
representation, and circuit structure and produces the discovered circuits without substantive human intervention.
Each architecture proposed during ideation founds a separate family of candidate programs. Later iterations improve
descendants of the first working implementation. Keeping the families separate at first gives each approach time
to mature, even if its earliest circuits are less efficient than those based on another approach. Periodically, leading
programs are shared across families, allowing successful ideas from different approaches to combine.
The search retains candidate programs and their evaluation results, tracking which family each belongs to and which
earlier program it was derived from. It also saves feedback on failures and short notes in which the LLM records
useful insights. These programs, results, and notes carry information between iterations; the sessions do not share a
continuously growing conversation.
Each iteration starts a new LLM agent session with the task specification, a parent program to improve, and its
evaluation feedback. The agent also receives alternative programs from the archive and their resource costs, brief
summaries of earlier attempts, and recorded insights where available. This context is limited; the agent never sees the
complete search history. Within the session, it may rewrite the program, run drafts on test inputs, query a classical
oracle for the required function on chosen inputs, and use the results to revise its design. It may also delegate focused
subtasks to subagents. The iteration ends with submission of a revised program. The evaluator rejects a circuit that
does not meet the task specification; a valid one is scored by itsT Q-cost and considered for retention in its family.
Thus the LLM is free to propose broad changes without having to judge their correctness: the evaluator supplies
an independent test of every completed design. Extended Data Figure 1 shows the ideation and rewrite calls and a
concrete feedback-and-repair cycle for a small finite-field inversion task.
To decide which valid candidates remain available within each family, the search retains circuits with different
trade-offs between Toffoli count and qubit count. We implement this selection with MAP-Elites [34], which divides the
(T, Q)resource plane into cells and keeps the circuit with the lowestT Q-cost in each cell. A space-efficient circuit can
therefore remain available for later development even when its higher Toffoli count gives it a worseT Q-cost.
Because the families preserve alternatives, progress need not be monotonic in either resource. The Gaussian lineage
in Extended Data Figure 2, for example, contains descendants that temporarily worsen Toffoli count or qubit count
before reaching a region of lowerT Q-cost. We report the lowestT Q-cost reached across all families at the end of the
search.

Consider an optimization problem over a finite fieldFq. An assignment x∈F nq produces m linear values Bx. Given
a set Fi ⊂F q of acceptable values for each constraint, letf (x) = |{i : (Bx)i ∈F i}| count the constraints satisfied byx;

Adaptive coefficient ranges.A naïve implementation would apply every BM update across all 36 possible coefficient

II.1. Divsteps
Rather than reorganize the binary-gcd constructions above, the search finds a new reversible prime-field inversion
architecture built ondivsteps[ 15, 53]. In the divstep recurrence of Equation (6), a small signed counterz, called the
half delta, replaces the repeated magnitude comparisons in Equation (55): the sign ofz and the parity of one value
alone determine the next update. We call the choice of update at a step itsdecision. Appendix A proves that every
decision can be computed exactly from the registers as they stand after the step. When a decision is cheap to decode,
the circuit decodes it from the updated state. Otherwise, the circuit stores the decision as a single bit on a fixed-size
history stack. We call the sequence of divsteps for one input awalk. The point adder uses this inversion twice, once to
form the slopeλ and once to erase it after the coordinate update. Each use runs a forward walk and a reverse walk,
for four walks in total.
Unlike the quotient-based extended Euclidean algorithm (EEA) of SI II.2, which divides one remainder by the other
at every iteration, a divstep never computes a multi-bit quotient or adds a multiple of one full register to another. A
divstep walk needs more steps than the quotient-based EEA needs divisions, but each step is much cheaper. Reversible
divsteps were previously developed over binary fields and polynomial rings [13, 54]. There, each step shrinks the
polynomial pair by one coefficient, and the freed space can hold that step’s record. Integer divsteps free no such fixed
space, so a stored history costs additional qubits. To our knowledge, this is the first reversible divstep inversion circuit
over a prime field.
We recall the notation of the main text. The walk state is(z, f, g, fv, gv), and each step applies one of the three
updates in Equation (6). Two bits label the update:s records whether the step swaps the roles off and g, and b
records whether the incomingg is odd. To run a step backward, the circuit must knows and b, and the updated
state does not hold them explicitly. Cheap tests on a few bits of the updated state recovers at every step andb at
about two thirds of the steps. We call the remaining decisionsambiguous. Each ambiguous decision has adepthj, the
number of trailing zero bits of the updated coefficientf ′v, and we writef ′v = 2jq with q odd. A swap followed by a run
of ambiguous decisions is anexcursion, and the depth counts the steps of the excursion so far, with the swap as step 1.

decodes an ambiguous decision at depthj < jstore and stores one at depthj≥j store.
The cutoff jstore trades qubits for Toffolis: a higher cutoff decodes more and stores less. Atjstore = 0, the circuit

Branch decoders.A forward step computes b from the parity ofg and uses it to control the update. After the
update, g no longer holds that parity, so the step is left withb in a qubit of its own. Uncomputingb there would
require the decoder on the forward walk as well. The forward step instead disposes ofb in one of two ways. If the
decision is ambiguous at depthj≥j store, the step pushesb onto the history stack. Otherwise, the step removesb by
MBU: it measuresb in the X basis. The measurement frees the qubit but leaves a phase that depends onb and on the
measurement outcome.
The reverse walk is the uncomputation of the forward walk. Once the circuit has used the inverse, it undoes the
forward steps in the opposite order, which restoresw and returns the walk registers to zero. The matching reverse step
obtains b again, either by popping the stack or by applying the tests and decoders to the updated state. It applies the
phase correction for the measurement outcome and usesb to choose which update to undo. The restoredg then holds
b as its parity, so a CNOT from the low bit ofg clears it. Decoders therefore appear only in reverse steps. A walk that
stores more decisions than the stack holds overflows it and fails.

Early stopping.A walk terminates when g reaches zero: at that pointf = ±1and the inverse can be read fromfv.
Different inputs terminate at different steps, typically between steps 505 and 530 (Figure 3(b)), but the circuit runs a
fixed schedule of 533 steps. The steps after termination must leave the state unchanged. Left alone, the recurrence
would treat g = 0as an even value, so it would keep doublingfv and incrementing z until both outgrow their registers.
The circuit therefore freezes a terminated walk. Each forward step tests whetherg = 0. On the first step where the
test succeeds, the circuit sets a one-qubit frozen flag, and from then on the flag disables every update. The reverse
walk must clear the flag at that same step. A frozen state looks identical at every later step, so the state alone cannot
reveal when the walk froze. The circuit writes the step index into a small register when it sets the flag. Each reverse
step compares the register with its own index, and a match clears both the flag and the register.
This machinery costs Toffolis at every step: a test that every bit ofg is zero, and one more control on every update.
The test is most expensive in the early steps, whereg is widest and termination is least likely. The first 498 steps omit
the test, the flag, and the flag’s controls. A walk that terminates within those steps fails. The measured probability is
1.4×10 −6 per inversion, and Table S4 uses3×10−6 as an upper bound.
A walk whose counterz outgrows its register also fails, with probability9× 10−8 per inversion; the two events give
the early-stopping row of Table S4.

coefficient claims positions vacated by its shrinking partner. The width and position of every value at every step are
fixed when the circuit is built. A walk in which a value outgrows its assigned width fails; this event is the integer-size

III. NUMERICAL FUNCTION EVALUATION
Elementary-function evaluation has been a central problem in numerical computing since the earliest mathematical
software libraries. The routines conventionally collected inlibm, the mathematical-function library supplied by C and
Unix systems, evaluate roots, exponentials, logarithms, and trigonometric functions by combining range reduction, table
lookup, polynomial approximation, and iterative refinement [57]. These functions also appear throughout quantum
algorithms. They determine controlled rotation angles in quantum linear-systems algorithms [12, 58], set interaction
phases in real-space Hamiltonian simulation [59, 60], and enter the amplitudes prepared for Hamiltonian simulation [4].
These classical techniques cannot simply be ported into coherent quantum circuits. Data-dependent choices must be
compiled into a fixed gate sequence, and all temporary state must ultimately be returned to|0⟩. The latter reversibility
constraint is particularly costly for numerical routines because many classical methods proceed through a long sequence
of large, high-precision intermediate values. An ordinary implementation can overwrite each value and reuse the same
storage as soon as the next value is formed. A reversible implementation must instead retain enough information to
reconstruct the overwritten value, or recompute it when clearing the circuit. Consequently, composing individually
efficient routines can accumulate many large live registers and may require running the complete computation backward
after its result has been consumed. Iterative Newton-style methods repeatedly replace one full-precision estimate with
another, so their reversible implementations carry large intermediate registers through every step [61]. Floating-point
representations add exponent bits to every such register to track a wide range of magnitudes [62]. The approximation,
fixed-point representation, and uncomputation schedule therefore become inseparable parts of circuit design [12].
We consider three numerical subroutines: Harrow–Hassidim–Lloyd (HHL) rotation-angle evaluation (arcsin),
Coulomb-potential evaluation (1/

ditive fixed-point map|x⟩ |k⟩ 7→ |x⟩ |k+ef(x)⟩, with a uniform error bound over its promised domain [63]. The additive
form serves both common uses of the result. An ordinary accumulatork holds the value for later bitwise-controlled
operations, while a phase-gradient accumulator converts the addition directly into a phase.
Our baselines use the piecewise-polynomial construction of Häner, Roetteler, and Svore (HRS) [12]. HRS partitions
the domain, fits a low-degree polynomial on each interval, and coherently loads the selected polynomial’s coefficients.
It evaluates the polynomial in general fixed-point arithmetic by Horner’s rule, which uses one multiplication per degree.
It adds the result to the output and reverses the computation to clear its temporary registers. Its cost has four sources:
locating an interval with arbitrary boundaries; the wide multiplications of the Horner polynomial-evaluation stage;
the registers that hold intermediate products and the polynomial value; and the reverse pass that erases the loaded
coefficients. For each task we tune the polynomial degree, fixed-point format, and published recomputation trade-offs,
and widen each interval until it reaches the error bound. We then use the complete gate-level implementation with the
lowestT Q-cost.
The three discovered circuits still implement piecewise-polynomial approximations. They keep the HRS approximation
family but reorganize it for reversible execution, addressing the four costs in turn. They choose finer partitions whose
intervals can be identified from the input bits. No comparison is then needed, and a polynomial of lower degree on
fewer input bits suffices: the HHL and Gaussian circuits replace cubics by quadratics, and the Coulomb circuit replaces
quadratics by lines. Where the intervals differ in width, as in the HHL circuit, a rearrangement of the input bits maps
them into one shared local coordinate, so that one arithmetic kernel serves every interval. This is the conditional
routing of the main text: a cheap, interval-dependent rearrangement of the data surrounds one shared kernel. The
arithmetic accumulates directly into its destination, avoiding registers for full products and for the function value.
MBU (Methods) then clears the loaded coefficients without reversing the lookup.
One supporting technique makes these choices safe. An offline search fits the coefficients to the exact quantized and
truncated integer computation that the circuit performs, rather than rounding an ideal real-valued approximation
afterward. Coefficient widths, omitted product columns, shifts, truncation, and rounding therefore share one end-to-end
error budget.

For arbitrary, unstructured table data, QROM has no pattern to exploit: in the worst case, every address may store
an unrelated word. However, this table is not arbitrary. Its Pauli operators and coefficients come from a physical
system with exact spin symmetries and repeated orbital environments, so many coefficients are equal and many Pauli
operators are related. We therefore choose the table to suit the circuits. Two degrees of freedom make this possible.
First, permuting terms together with their coefficients and Pauli operators leaves Equation (68) unchanged. Second,
many different alias tables realize the same rounded distribution. The preprocessing uses the first freedom to group
Pauli operators related by fixed Clifford transformations into blocks, shorteningSelect. It uses the second to place
equal alias words into repeated patterns, shortening QROM.
Frame-blockSelect.Many Pauli operators in this Hamiltonian are related by fixed Clifford transformations,
which we callframes. Suppose, for example, that two commuting frames generate four related Pauli operators from
one representative. Instead of selecting four operators independently, the circuit selects the representative once and
uses two bits of the address (the selected row index) to control the two frames. More generally,k such bits can identify
up to2 k operators in a block. The circuit pays for one block representative plus its controlled frames, rather than
every Pauli operator separately.
The circuit applies a frame by conjugation. It appliesσ† to the system register under a control, then the representative
P, then σ under the same control. The result isσP σ† when the control is set andP otherwise. Each frame is applied
once for the whole table rather than once per block. Blocks that use the same frames are contiguous, so a few
comparisons of the address with fixed boundaries determine which frames are active.
These relations have a physical origin: the model contains two equivalent spin channels, two similar iron sites, and
corresponding orbitals on repeated parts of the cluster. These repetitions create Pauli operators that are related by
a fixed transformation. Each frame is a permutation or change of basis of the spin-orbitals. The Jordan–Wigner
mapping turns it into a fixed Clifford transformation of the 40 system qubits.
Two of the frames used here come from exact spin symmetries of the Hamiltonian. Because the model treats the
spin-up and spin-down channels identically,σαβ exchanges the spin-up and spin-down modes of every spatial orbital.
Spin-rotation symmetry produces a second pairing after the Jordan–Wigner mapping:σXY exchanges the X and
Y factors and adjusts the accompanyingZ strings required by that mapping. Both transformations preserve the
corresponding coefficient magnitudes.
The other five frames come from parts of the cluster that resemble each other; unlike the two spin frames, they
are not symmetries of the full Hamiltonian. The frameσFe is the approximate left–right reflection of the cluster,
which exchanges the two iron centers and their surrounding orbitals. The frameσ′Fe combines this reflection with
several local orbital swaps and matches Pauli operators that the bare reflection misses. The framesσthio, σ3d, and σµS
exchange corresponding orbitals on the terminal thiolates, the iron3d shells, and the bridging sulfur atoms. These
sites have similar but not identical electronic environments: the permutation often maps one term’s Pauli operator
onto another’s, but the coefficients differ. Only the Pauli relationPℓ′ = σPℓσ† must hold exactly;wℓ′ and wℓ remain
independent entries in the alias table.
The preprocessing thus finds a commuting family of seven frames and orders related Pauli operators into blocks
whose sizes are powers of two, so that address bits index the operators within a block. The 41,052 terms form 3,221
blocks. Selecting the block representatives costs 3,327 Toffolis. Applying the seven frames and determining which are
active costs 534. Figure S4 shows the resulting structure.

V. NONABELIAN LATTICE-GAUGE SIMULATION
In Hamiltonian lattice gauge theory, space is replaced by a grid. A gauge-field configuration assigns a group element
Ui(x)to each directed edge, or link, from sitexin directioni. This element describes how the gauge field transports a
state between neighboring sites; traversing the link backward contributes its inverseUi(x)−1. Multiplying the links
around a closed path therefore describes the net transport along that loop. Although this product depends on an
arbitrary convention at each site (the gauge), the real part of its matrix trace does not, and these loop traces form the
magnetic energy [73]. Because the gauge group is nonabelian, its elements need not commute, so the links must be
multiplied in the order in which the path traverses them.
We consider the group-register formulation of Hamiltonian lattice gauge theory [73], in which a register holds the
group element of each link. The Hamiltonian separates into an electric (kinetic) termHE and a magnetic (potential)
term HB. A Trotter step evolves these terms separately. Here we implement the magnetic evolutione−i∆tHB for the
improved Hamiltonian of Carenaet al.[74]. The smallest loop contributing toHB goes around one square face of the
lattice and is called aplaquette; the standard magnetic term sums a trace over these four-link loops. The improved
Hamiltonian adds weighted2× 1rectangles, which cancel the leading discretization error. Thus the magnetic evolution
requires the same basic operation at many locations: follow a short closed path, multiply its links in order, extract a
trace, and add the weighted result to the phase. The compilation task studied here is one application of the magnetic
evolution to the whole lattice, which we call themagnetic layer.

Reusing loop products.The baseline constructs each loop product from the identity and then reverses the
construction. Neighboring loops, however, often share a sequence of factors. The discovered circuit therefore retains
W after one trace evaluation and edits it into the product needed for the next, reusing the shared factors instead of
rebuilding them.
More loops share factors than first appears, because the real trace is invariant under cyclic rotation and inversion:
f(h 1h2 · · ·hn) =f(h 2 · · ·hnh1) =f((h 1h2 · · ·hn)−1).(86)
The first equality is cyclicity of the matrix trace; the second follows because these matrices are unitary andf takes the
real part. For example, to changeabcde into abcxy, the circuit right-multiplies bye−1 and d−1 to cancel the old suffix,
then appends x and y, retaining the common prefixabc. Inverting W, which costs 21 Toffolis, makes the other end of
the product available for the same operation. Cyclic rotations and inversion also give several equivalent ways to write
each loop, which we call itsrepresentatives, all with the same value off. As Figure S5(a) illustrates, a good choice can
expose a longer segment shared with the next loop. A classical dynamic program chooses the representatives and the
order in which the loops are evaluated, minimizing the cost of transformingW between consecutive representatives.
The chosen schedule uses 3,136 link multiplications and 480 inversions ofW for the 576 loops, about 5.4 multiplications
per loop. By comparison, building and reversing each loop separately takes 6 multiplications for a plaquette and 10 for
a rectangle.
