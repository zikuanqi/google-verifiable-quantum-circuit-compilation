Leveraging verifiable structure in quantum circuits
to find more efficient compilations with agentic AI
Tanuj Khattar, Nicholas C. Rubin, Craig Gidney, Michael Broughton, Guang Hao Low, and Ryan Babbush
Google Quantum AI, Santa Barbara, CA 93111, United States
(Dated: October 22, 2026)
We discuss an effective paradigm of utilizing AI agents to better compile quantum circuits when
one can constrain bottleneck subroutines to have some form of classically verifiable structure. For
example, when critical subroutines are composed exclusively of reversible classical logic, phasing
operations, and measurement based uncomputation, one can efficiently simulate execution for classical
inputs. This property facilitates efficient certification of approximate circuit synthesis via a form
of fuzz testing, provided suitable assumptions on the input distribution. While this pattern was
recently exploited to improve quantum cryptanalysis circuits, here we highlight the flexibility of the
broader approach by discussing three important applications. (1) The computation of1/√x that
bottlenecks real space simulations of the Coulomb operator such as those important for modeling
inertial confinement fusion reactors. (2) The decoding of Reed-Solomon codes which bottlenecks the
decoded quantum interferometry algorithm when used to approximately solve the optimal polynomial
intersection problem. And (3) the quantum look-up tables and controlled application of Givens
rotations that bottleneck the most performant quantum algorithms for simulating chemistry in
second quantization. We discuss reference implementations of these algorithms and how they can
be constrained and verified in the interest of seeding a Kaggle competition designed to accelerate
AI-based research into the optimization of their compilation.

I. INTRODUCTION
Compiling a fault-tolerant quantum algorithm into a concrete circuit requires navigating a large design space of
arithmetic decompositions, ancilla-management schedules, and precision trade-offs. Until recently, researchers explored
this space by hand, often spending months optimizing a single subroutine and proving its correctness step by step.
Automated circuit optimizers [1–3] help locally, but they preserve exact unitaries on small subcircuits rather than
changing the algorithm or its approximations. AI coding agents [4] can automate this search if a fast test harness
checks each candidate circuit and scores its resource cost in a closed loop. For general quantum circuits, checking
whether a candidate circuit matches a target unitary is QMA-complete [5] and intractable on a classical computer
above roughly 40 qubits. Fortunately, in many fault-tolerant quantum algorithms, the subroutines that dominate
the cost do not use arbitrary quantum interference internally [6–12]. Instead, they use only reversible classical gates,
diagonal phase gates, and measurement-based uncomputation. This structure lets a classical simulator efficiently check
their approximate correctness on sampled computational basis inputs.
Ref. [13] used this method in March 2026 to estimate the cost of breaking 256-bit elliptic curve cryptography on the
secp256k1 curve with Shor’s algorithm. To follow responsible disclosure, the authors withheld the explicit circuits.
Instead, they released an open-source simulator and test harness with a zero-knowledge proof that their hidden circuit
passed the randomized tests. Soon after Ref. [13] appeared, theecdsa.fail challenge took their simulator and test
harness and built a public competition for secp256k1 point addition circuits. The harness checked the approximate
correctness of each submission in seconds and ranked it by its qubit and Toffoli counts. This fast verification let the
organizers accept submissions without trusting the participants. Over 100 participants, most without a quantum
computing background, used AI coding agents to further reduce the Toffoli counts by roughly half [14].
In this paper, we apply this workflow to three benchmarks beyond cryptanalysis and use them to seed a Kaggle
competition. We make three main contributions:
1. We release kickmix, an open-source C++20 and Python package in Qualtran [15] that builds and simulates
circuits with over108 gates on one CPU core.
2. We describe a non-interactive verifier that seeds its test inputs with a salted hash of the submitted circuit. We
also bound the distance between the implemented and ideal output states by the circuit’s failure rate on those
inputs.
3. We define three benchmarks and provide a reference implementation for each of them:
3
• Phasing by1/√x for first-quantized simulations of Coulomb interactions, such as inertial confinement fusion [9].
• Reed-Solomon syndrome decoding for decoded quantum interferometry (DQI) on the optimal polynomial
intersection problem [12, 16].
• Quantum lookup tables and Majorana rotations for block encodings of electronic-structure Hamiltonians [11].
Because classical fuzz testing applies only to kickmix circuits, we restrict each benchmark to the kickmix subroutine
that dominates the algorithm’s cost. When that subroutine interacts with non-kickmix parts such as arbitrary state
preparation, as in Benchmark III, we price those parts with analytical cost formulas. This pricing ensures that the
benchmark score still reflects the full algorithm.
Section II defines the kickmix gate set, describes thekickmix software package, and derives the state-distance bound
from fuzz testing. Section III describes the background, reference circuit, test distribution, and score for each benchmark.
Section IV discusses how circuit testing might change as AI agents improve. The appendices derive the DFTHC
block-encoding circuits and the polynomial approximations in the1/√xbaseline.
II. KICKMIX CIRCUITS AND FUZZ TESTING VIA FIAT-SHAMIR HEURISTIC
Optimizing quantum circuits with AI agents requires a fast, reliable way to check whether a candidate circuit works.
For general quantum circuits, classical simulation takes exponential time, and deciding whether two circuits implement
the same operation is coNP-complete classically and QMA-complete quantumly [5]. Ref. [13] introduced a restricted
family of quantum circuits, calledkickmixcircuits, built exclusively from reversible classical gates, diagonal phase
operations, and measurement-based uncomputation. Because every instruction in this family maps any computational
basis state to a single computational basis state up to a scalar phase, a classical simulator can run a kickmix circuit on
computational basis inputs in time linear in gate count.
Although full quantum algorithms rely on quantum interference across superpositions, many of the subroutines that
dominate fault-tolerant resource estimates (such as integer arithmetic, quantum lookup tables, and syndrome decoders)
can be written as kickmix circuits [9, 12, 13, 17]. This structure suggests checking candidate compilations by testing
them on randomly sampled classical inputs. However, doing so requires care: one must check in each problem context
that approximate correctness on sampled basis states guarantees high fidelity on the quantum states used by the
algorithm. When this holds, combining classical trajectory simulation with the Fiat-Shamir heuristic [13, 18] turns
random-input fuzz testing into a public, non-interactive verification protocol that requires no secret test set. To run
this verification loop on subroutines exceeding108 gates, we releasekickmix as an open-source C++20 and Python
package inside Qualtran [15]. On a single CPU core,kickmix builds circuits at2× 107 instructions/s and simulates
them at6.0× 1010 instructions/s, constructing an86M-gate Decoded Quantum Interferometry circuit in less than
5seconds and checking102,400syndrome-decoder shots in150seconds.
A. The kickmix gate set
When a kickmix circuit runs on ann-qubit computational basis input|x⟩, no instruction creates a superposition over
multiple computational basis states. A classical simulator therefore needs to store only one bit per qubit wire, one bit
per classical register wire, and one scalar output phase. Specifically, every instruction in the kickmix gate set falls into
one of four classes:
1. Reversible classical gates (X, CX, CCX, and SWAP): Permute computational basis states by updating the stored
qubit bits directly.
2. Diagonal phase gates (Z, CZ, CCZ, Z_POW, and NEG): Leave the qubit bits unchanged and update the tracked
output phase whenever all control qubits hold|1⟩.
3. Measurement and reset gates (HMR and R): Measure a qubit in theX basis into a classical bit and reset it to|0⟩
(HMR), or reset a qubit directly to|0⟩(R).
4. Classical bit operations (BIT_STORE0, BIT_STORE1, and BIT_INVERT): Set classical register bits to0or1, or
invert them in place.

qubits.
B. The kickmix software package in Qualtran
The kickmix library is implemented in C++20 with Python bindings inside Qualtran [15]. Inspired by the bit-packed
stabilizer engine of Stim [19], kickmix is built around two design principles that let humans and AI agents iterate on
108-gate circuits in seconds.
First, performance drives every layer of circuit construction, serialization, and simulation. Standard Python circuit
libraries allocate a separate heap object for every gate and simulate one input at a time, which becomes prohibitively
slow at10 8 gates. Instead, kickmix stores a circuit using a struct-of-arrays representation grouped by operand
count: one byte array of opcodes (OpType) alongside separate arrays for1-,2-, and3-qubit arguments, classical target
and condition bits, and rotation angles. Each1-byte opcode embeds bit flags indicating which operand arrays it
consumes, letting the simulator step sequential pointers through only the active arrays without padding every gate
to the worst-case size. Saving and loading a circuit in binary.kmb format transfers each array between memory
and disk as a contiguous binary block, avoiding per-gate text formatting and parsing. During simulation,kickmix
bit-packs64to512independent trajectories across CPU SIMD registers so each instruction updates an entire batch of
shots in parallel. The one exception isZ_POW: because each trajectory maintains its own128-bit fixed-precision phase
accumulator rather than a single sign bit, aZ_POW instruction cannot update the whole batch with a single bitwise
SIMD operation. Instead, it scans the active shot mask across all trajectories in the batch and adds the rotation angle
to each active shot sequentially.
Second, expressiveness guides the Python builder API so complex end-to-end algorithms remain compact and readable.
To eliminate the repetitive boilerplate that arises when writing multi-stage fault-tolerant algorithms with measurement-
based uncomputation,kickmixprovides four abstractions:
1. Automatic downgrading: Operations accept qubits, classical bits, and compile-time constants interchangeably,
automatically replacing classically controlled gates with lower-cost instructions, dropping no-ops onFalse
controls, and supporting block-scoped classical conditions.
2. NumPy-style broadcasting: Most primitive gates, measurements, and classical bit operations accept both single
wires and register arrays (km.array), broadcasting scalars against registers or operating elementwise across
registers, so a single builder call replaces an explicit loop over individual qubits.
3. Dynamic workspace allocation: The builder provides methods to allocate and free temporary quantum and
classical registers within a configurable qubit budget. Subroutines can use these methods to request and release
scratch space locally, and freed qubits are handed back out to later allocations.
4. Library of common subroutines: The builder provides a library of commonly used fault-tolerant subroutines,
including temporary-AND gates, QROM table lookups, adders, comparators, finite-field arithmetic, and context-
managed unary iteration, with paired compute and measurement-based uncompute variants.
5
kickmix also includes inspection utilities that generate circuit diagrams and interactive SVG flame charts showing
where Toffoli gates and qubits are spent across nested subroutines.
C. Fuzz testing via the Fiat-Shamir heuristic
To verify a candidate kickmix circuit, we simulate it on computational basis inputs usingkm.Simulator and compare
the resulting bitstrings and phases against a classical reference. When the input domain is small, we can test every
basis state exhaustively to certify exact correctness on all inputs. When the input domain is too large to enumerate,
fuzz testing on a random sample of inputs instead certifies approximate correctness by bounding the probabilityε that
the circuit fails on a random input. Because verification reduces to bounding this failure rateε, the same procedure
naturally accommodates approximations in circuit compilations that are correct on almost all inputs, such as dropping
low-weight carry bits, truncating intermediate registers, or bounding iteration counts to save Toffolis [7, 13].

To prevent a submission from hard-coding outputs on a fixed test set, our verifier generates challenge inputs via the
Fiat-Shamir heuristic [13, 18]. Given a serialized circuit fileC, the verifier hashes the raw bytes ofC with SHA-256
and uses that256-bit digest to seed a deterministic pseudorandom generator. Changing even a single byte ofC yields
a completely new SHA-256 digest and a fresh set ofN challenge inputs. In a competition setting, we also combine
the circuit’s SHA-256 digest with a private salt to derive the generator seed so that participants cannot perform
seed grinding by repeatedly adding no-op instructions to search for a seed that generates easier test inputs. This
construction makes verification non-interactive, deterministic, and reproducible once the salt is published, without
maintaining large secret test sets.

III. KAGGLE COMPETITION
We are launching a public competition on Kaggle, an online platform for machine learning and algorithmic challenges,
featuring three benchmarks from fusion simulation, decoded quantum interferometry, and quantum chemistry. Each
benchmark isolates the kickmix subroutine that dominates the fault-tolerant cost of the full algorithm at a fixed
problem size. In our own runs with AI coding agents, we have found significant improvements over the previously
published baselines, and we expect further improvements remain to be discovered. For each benchmark, we commit to
our best internal run via its SHA-256 hash2 and will release our full solutions at the end of the competition. With
a $150,000 prize pool, we welcome anyone interested in AI-driven optimization, regardless of quantum computing
background, to try new ideas and push these benchmarks further. Below, we motivate each problem, describe the
baseline circuit from prior work, and specify the verification contract and score.
A. Benchmark I: Inverse square roots for Coulomb simulations with relevance to fusion reactor design

C. Benchmark III: QROM and Majorana rotations for quantum simulations of chemistry
1. Problem introduction and importance
Quantum circuits for state-of-the-art ground-state energy estimation of a second-quantized electronic-structure
Hamiltonian involve an important class of data-lookup circuits which are leading Toffoli cost. These costs are intimately
related to the cost of specifying the Hamiltonian and are thus important primitives for algorithms encoding in second
quantization. Thus their compilation is of interest beyond the benchmark provided here.
Key to many quantum algorithms is the specification of the operator being simulated. Our primary focus here will be
using the linear combination of unitary approach which represents an operator as a sum of unitaries multiplied by a
non-negative real weight. In the chemistry example considered the operator is the Hamiltonian and specifies how the
system interacts. For a system ofn-qubits the quantum chemistry Hamiltonian can be specified withO(n4)amount of
information. The goal of many modern quantum chemistry algorithms is to figure out how to get the Hamiltonian into
the quantum circuit for less thanO(n4)costly quantum operations by a combination of classical compression and
circuit design.
Our goal will be to specify the Hamiltonian in Eq.(45) in the lowest space-time volume possible using an LCU where
the unitaries are the rank-1 factorizations of the integral tensor. Almost all modern second quantized simulation
techniques leverage compression of the two-body component of Eq.(45) which corresponds to writing the four tensor
h(2)pqrs as a tensor network and use a rank-1 unitary representation to build the full operator. In this benchmark we fix
the tensor network to be that of Ref. [11] and require the user to find optimal dimensions of the tensor network that
achieve a good approximation ofh(2)pqrs and h(1)pq along with a small spacetime volume for the overall circuit. We cannot
ask for the entire compilation of Ref. [11] as that requires a verification framework beyond kickmix, but we will use a
proxy to ensure that users cannot artificially change the parameters to lower the space-time cost of the table lookups
over the other components of the algorithm. The combination of classical optimization of the tensor network and
how that leads to an efficient quantum circuit is representative of the type of large-scale computational workflow that
informs the optimal circuit design.

4. Verification of circuit for Boolean oracle
This is assumed to be a collection ofK kickmix circuits that implement the boolean oracleAnglesk. Any ancilla
qubits used are assumed to start in, and are returned to, the|0⟩ state. Each Anglesk oracle must declare named
registers ‘selection’ and ‘word_0’, ..., ‘word_J-1’. The selection register should be of size⌈log2(N +RB)⌉ qubits and
take on valuesa<N +RB in binary. The firstN select the one-body components of the Hamiltonian while the last
RB components select the two-particle part. Each ‘word_j’ register isβ qubits wide and holds one rotation angle as a
fixed point number in binary; the verifier readsβ and J from these registers. The fixed point number is between[0, 2)
and thus has a single integer bit andβ− 1mantissa bits. All other qubits are workspace that must start and end in
|0⟩, and Z_POW instructions are not allowed, so every phase is a multiple ofπ. The family of oracles is simulated for
every input in the ‘selection’ register, once forward and once in reverse, with64shots per address, and we verify the
construction of the binary representation of angles for Givens rotations. The user has freedom to specify how the
boolean function should work, bits of precision, ordering of the Tree for Givens rotations, and tensor factorizations of
the Hamiltonian. All should be optimized to minimize the cost function heuristic designed to minimize the spacetime
footprint of the overall block encoding algorithm.

IV. OUTLOOK
The benchmarks in this paper evaluate flat gate streams using logical qubit and Toffoli counts. While this setup gives
AI agents a fast inner loop for subroutine optimization, moving toward end-to-end fault-tolerant compilation suggests
two main directions:
1. Compiling directly to a target QEC architecture: Logical qubit and Toffoli counts are coarse proxies for physical
hardware cost. In practice, hardware capabilities dictate the choice of quantum error-correcting (QEC) code.
That code choice then determines how the architecture implements logical gates and what overheads arise from
magic-state distillation, qubit routing, and reaction time [7, 10, 12, 29, 30]. Future benchmarks can keepkickmix
simulation to check logical correctness while scoring submissions with a QEC cost model for a target architecture.
AI agents can then co-optimize the logical arithmetic, qubit layout, and gate schedule to minimize physical
qubits and runtime.
2. Machine-checkable proofs of circuit correctness: Classical simulation applies only to kickmix circuits, and a
flat .kmb gate stream only tests a single problem size. Many quantum subroutines, such as state preparation,
quantum signal processing, and fermionic rotations, create superpositions internally and cannot be checked by a
kickmix simulator. When human researchers optimize these subroutines, they design parameterized constructions
for any input sizen and prove their correctness and cost bounds symbolically. As AI reasoning models and proof
assistants such as Lean [31] mature, submissions could pair a circuit generator with a proof of its correctness
and cost. Because a proof assistant checks algebraic identities rather than simulating basis states, it can certify
arbitrary quantum unitaries regardless of Hilbert space dimension. This shift would let AI agents optimize
general quantum algorithms end to end and discover parameterized constructions that hold across all problem
sizes.
Together, fast verifiers, architecture-aware cost models, and formal proofs can democratize fault-tolerant compilation,
opening quantum algorithm design to researchers and engineers across all fields.
